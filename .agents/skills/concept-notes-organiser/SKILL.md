# Concept Notes Organiser

## Purpose

Help a product team turn fragmented notes, workshop outputs, hypotheses and early ideas into a coherent, reviewable concept without losing the original thinking.

The skill organises ideas; it does not make strategic decisions on the team's behalf. It must distinguish what the source material says from interpretations, hypotheses and recommendations.

## When to use

Use this skill when a team member provides:
- Workshop notes, brainstorms, sticky-note exports or meeting notes.
- Early product ideas or concept explorations.
- A collection of related observations, opportunities and possible capabilities.
- An existing concept document that needs clearer structure.
- A request to derive milestones, stages or a roadmap narrative from an idea.

Do not use it to turn unrelated notes into one concept. If the material contains multiple distinct concepts, separate them and explain the proposed grouping.

## Working principles

1. **Preserve before improving.** Retain the source's terminology, intent, distinctions and level of certainty.
2. **Do not invent missing facts.** Mark gaps as open questions. Do not silently add market evidence, user needs, dates, technical feasibility or business commitments.
3. **Separate evidence from interpretation.** Label source-derived observations, team hypotheses, synthesis and recommendations.
4. **Keep the user's outcome central.** Organise around what someone is trying to achieve, not just a feature inventory.
5. **Make the logic visible.** Show how observations connect to problems, opportunities, concepts, capabilities and outcomes.
6. **Keep uncertainty useful.** Preserve competing ideas and tensions when they matter; do not force false consensus.
7. **Use plain language.** Avoid jargon unless it is part of the team's vocabulary; define specialist terms when needed.
8. **Be proportionate.** Do not force every section into a small or immature idea. Use only the structure supported by the material.

## Workflow

### 1. Understand the material

Identify:
- The apparent topic or concept.
- The intended audience or user, if stated.
- The problem, need or opportunity, if stated.
- The desired outcome.
- Existing constraints, dependencies and assumptions.
- Decisions already made versus ideas still being explored.

If the source is ambiguous, proceed with a clearly labelled working interpretation and list the ambiguity. Ask a clarification only when the ambiguity prevents useful organisation.

### 2. Preserve the raw thinking

Create a short **Source capture** section:
- Key statements and ideas, paraphrased faithfully.
- Important phrases worth retaining verbatim.
- Distinct viewpoints or conflicting suggestions.
- Explicit decisions and unresolved questions.

Do not present a synthesis as if it were a direct statement from the team.

### 3. Organise the concept

Use the following structure where supported by the source:

# [Concept name]
* [Short descriptor or domain, if known]

## 1. Concept in brief
A concise explanation of the idea, who it may serve and the change it aims to create. If these are not clear, state what is known and what remains unclear.

## 2. The opportunity
- **Current situation:** What happens today?
- **User need or tension:** What is difficult, missing or unnecessarily complex?
- **Opportunity:** What could become possible?
- **Why now:** Include only if the source supports it.

Do not assume that an opportunity is a validated user problem.

## 3. Product thesis
Write a short thesis describing the proposed shift. Prefer:
- “From [current way of working] to [desired way of working].”
- Explain what the user defines or controls and what the product/system enables or coordinates.

Keep this as a hypothesis unless it has been validated.

## 4. What makes the concept distinct?
Identify the combination of enabling factors, capabilities or principles that may make the concept meaningful. Separate:
- What is already possible or established (only if supported).
- What the proposed concept brings together.
- What is genuinely novel or still needs validation.

Avoid unsupported claims such as “first”, “only” or “unique”.

## 5. Concept model
Describe the main conceptual layers, objects or steps. Use the source's own model where one exists. For each layer, explain:
- Its purpose.
- What information or capability it contains.
- How it relates to the other layers.

Keep distinct concepts distinct. For example, do not collapse a user's goal, a strategy, a permission policy and an execution plan into one object.

## 6. Core experiences or capability areas
Group related capabilities into a small number of meaningful experience areas. For each:
- **Name**
- **Purpose**
- **User question it answers**
- **Capabilities or interactions**
- **Dependencies / risks**, if present in the source

Do not turn every brainstormed feature into a committed capability.

## 7. Example scenario
If the source supports it, show one concrete user scenario:
1. User context or trigger.
2. User's desired outcome.
3. Information or choices presented.
4. User decision and authorization.
5. System action or support.
6. Result, feedback and ongoing control.

Clearly label illustrative examples as examples, not confirmed product behaviour.

## 8. Milestones
Create an outcome-led hierarchy using the milestone format below.

## 9. Assumptions, risks and open questions
Separate:
- **Assumptions:** Things being treated as true but not yet established.
- **Risks / constraints:** Factors that could prevent or weaken the concept.
- **Open questions:** Decisions or evidence still needed.

Where possible, phrase assumptions so they can be tested.

## 10. Decisions and next steps
Record:
- Decisions already made.
- Recommendations for discussion (not decisions).
- The smallest useful next steps to reduce uncertainty.

### 4. Write milestones in the agreed format

The canonical sentence format is:

> By [year], [who] can [do what] without [what they do today].

A good milestone describes a meaningful change in a person's ability or experience. It is not a feature delivery statement.

**Rules**
- **By [year]:** Use a year only when the source or team has provided one. If no year is known, retain [year] or label a suggested horizon as *proposed*, never as a commitment.
- **[Who]:** Name the user or actor specifically enough to be meaningful. Do not invent a target audience.
- **[Can do what]:** Describe an observable outcome or new capability from the user's perspective.
- **Without [today's friction]:** Describe the current manual, fragmented, confusing or burdensome behaviour that the milestone aims to reduce. Use only friction supported by the source; otherwise label it as a hypothesis.
- Keep one primary outcome per milestone.
- Prefer clear, testable language over broad claims such as “seamlessly”, “effortlessly” or “intelligently”.
- Do not write milestones as “build a dashboard”, “launch an AI agent” or “integrate protocol X”. Those are potential deliverables or enablers, not user outcomes.

**Milestone hierarchy**

A. **North Star milestone** — the larger change the concept aims to enable.

B. **Experience milestones** — the user-facing capabilities that make the North Star possible.

C. **Enabling milestones** — intelligence, infrastructure, trust, safety, data or operational capabilities required to support the experience. Write these from the user or operator outcome where possible; do not disguise internal engineering tasks as user milestones.

For each milestone, include:
- **Milestone statement:** the canonical sentence.
- **Type:** North Star / Experience / Enabling.
- **Stage or horizon:** only if supported; otherwise “To be sequenced”.
- **Why it matters:** one sentence.
- **Dependencies:** only known or clearly labelled as likely.
- **Confidence:** Source-backed / Team hypothesis / Suggested framing.

**Group milestones into stages only when there is a meaningful maturity progression.** A useful pattern may be:
- Understand and define.
- Compose and control.
- Delegate and maintain.

This is an optional pattern, not a mandatory roadmap. Do not force dates or stages onto the material.

### 5. Quality-check the synthesis

Before presenting, check:
- Does every major statement have a clear status: source fact, team hypothesis, synthesis or recommendation?
- Have important distinctions and disagreements been preserved?
- Does the concept explain a user outcome rather than only list features?
- Does each milestone follow the exact sentence structure?
- Are dates clearly sourced or explicitly proposed?
- Are milestones distinct, non-duplicative and ordered by dependency or maturity?
- Have unsupported claims, assumed user needs and invented capabilities been removed or labelled?
- Can a teammate trace the structure back to the original notes?

## Output format

Default to a concise but useful Markdown document:

1. Concept in brief
2. Opportunity
3. Product thesis
4. Differentiation / enabling factors
5. Concept model
6. Core experiences / capability areas
7. Example scenario (if supported)
8. Concept milestones
   - North Star
   - Experience milestones
   - Enabling milestones
   - Optional maturity stages
9. Assumptions, risks and open questions
10. Decisions and next steps

Add a final **What I changed / what I did not assume** note when the source is especially fragmented:
- What was grouped or clarified.
- What terminology was retained.
- What remains uncertain.
- Any suggested framing that needs team agreement.

## Interaction behaviour

- If the user says “organise these notes”, produce the structured synthesis directly.
- If the user says “extract milestones”, focus on milestones and explain their hierarchy.
- If the user says “challenge this concept”, identify gaps, contradictions and untested assumptions before proposing changes.
- If the user says “make it shorter”, preserve the thesis, milestone hierarchy and critical uncertainties.
- If several concepts appear, propose a separation before merging them.
- Do not ask the team to fill out a long questionnaire. Ask at most one essential clarification; otherwise proceed and label uncertainty.

## Example milestone pattern

**North Star**
> By 2028, [defined user group] can [achieve the larger outcome] without [the current fragmented or manual approach].

**Experience**
> By [year], [user group] can [understand / define / compare / control / verify a meaningful outcome] without [specific current friction].

**Enabling**
> By [year], [user or operator] can [rely on / inspect / recover / govern a required capability] without [specific current limitation].

These are templates, not claims about a particular product. Replace every bracketed phrase with source-supported content.
